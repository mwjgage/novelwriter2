# Chapter 17: Rogue Waves, Meteotsunamis, and Seas Gone Mad

For centuries, sailors described waves taller than a building and were mostly told they were exaggerating. In 1995 a laser on a North Sea platform settled the argument.

1. **The Draupner wave was the first rogue wave measured by an instrument.** On 1 January 1995, a laser sensor on a gas platform in the North Sea recorded a wave of 25.6 metres (84 feet) at a time when the surrounding seas averaged about 12 metres. The platform was built to survive a 20-metre wave.

2. **Before that, rogue waves were sailors' stories.** Ship reports of walls of water had circulated for centuries, and many oceanographers assumed they were exaggerations.

3. **An ESA satellite project found a lot of them.** The MaxWave project, using radar data from ESA's ERS satellites in 2000, identified about ten waves above 25 metres in three weeks over the global oceans, though the findings are still discussed.

4. **A buoy in 2013 recorded the largest significant wave height ever.** On 4 February 2013 in the North Atlantic, between Iceland and the UK, a buoy measured a significant wave height of 19 metres, which is the average of the highest third of waves. The WMO validated it in 2016.

5. **Hurricane Ivan produced a 27.7-metre wave.** Sensors on the seabed of the Gulf of Mexico recorded a maximum individual wave of 27.7 metres (91 feet) crest to trough in September 2004, with estimates that taller ones occurred near the eyewall.

6. **A cruise ship was hit by a rogue wave in 2010.** The *Louis Majesty* was struck in the Mediterranean on 3 March 2010, off Spain, with waves breaking windows on a high deck. Two passengers died.

7. **The Agulhas Current is notorious.** Off South Africa's east coast, a strong current runs southwest while storms push waves northeast. The opposition steepens the waves, and ships have reported damage there since the 1970s.

8. **A meteotsunami is a tsunami made by weather.** A fast-moving pressure disturbance or squall line can push the sea surface and generate a wave that resembles a tsunami, though no earthquake is involved.

9. **A meteotsunami hit Nagasaki Bay in 1979.** On 31 March, a wave reportedly about 5 metres high struck, killing three people. The local name for such events is *abiki*.

10. **Daytona Beach had one in 1992.** On 3 July, a 3-metre wave reached the shore and injured about 75 people, with damage to cars on the beach, and it was later traced to a squall line over the ocean.

11. **Lake Michigan had one in 1954.** On 26 June, a sudden surge at Chicago swept anglers off piers and killed eight. It's one of the best-known meteotsunami events on the Great Lakes.

12. **Ciutadella harbour on Menorca has a local word for them.** *Rissaga* waves hit the long, narrow inlet and can surge several metres within minutes, an effect amplified by the shape of the harbour.

13. **The Edmund Fitzgerald sank on Lake Superior on 10 November 1975.** All 29 crew died in a storm that produced waves of about 10 metres. The cause remains debated, and treat the competing explanations as **[THEORY]**.

14. **El Faro was lost in Hurricane Joaquin.** The cargo ship sank on 1 October 2015 with all 33 people aboard. Its voyage data recorder, recovered from 15,000 feet down, gave investigators audio from the bridge.

15. **Sea smoke rises from freezing air over warm water.** When air well below freezing flows across open water, the water evaporates into the cold and condenses into wisps. It's common in the Arctic and over the Great Lakes in winter.

16. **The Grand Banks are among the foggiest waters in the world.** Cold Labrador Current and warm Gulf Stream meet off Newfoundland. The area sees fog on well over 100 days a year.

17. **Swells can cross an ocean.** In 1963, Walter Munk and colleagues tracked waves from Southern Ocean storms across the Pacific to Alaska, a distance of more than 10,000 kilometres.

18. **Storm surge, not wind, kills most people.** In hurricanes, the rise of sea level pushes water inland and drowns people. That's why forecasters stress surge in evacuations.

19. **Waterspouts come in two kinds.** Tornadic waterspouts form from supercell tornadoes crossing water. Fair-weather waterspouts are more common and form over warm water under growing cumulus clouds.

20. **The Beaufort scale was devised for sailors.** Francis Beaufort, a Royal Navy officer, created the scale in 1805 by describing what a ship's sails did at different wind strengths. It was later adapted to describe conditions on land.
