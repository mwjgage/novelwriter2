# Chapter 15: Derechos, Downbursts, and Winds That Flatten Forests

Wind is hard to film, because you only see what it moves. These are the events where it moved a lot: grain bins, forests, aircraft, and one very fast jumbo jet.

1. **The strongest wind gust ever measured outside a tornado was 408 km/h.** It hit Barrow Island, off Western Australia, during Severe Tropical Cyclone Olivia on 10 April 1996. The Bureau of Meteorology flagged the reading as suspect at first, and the WMO ratified it in 2010 after checking the instrument.

2. **For decades the record belonged to a mountaintop in New Hampshire.** On 12 April 1934 the observatory on Mount Washington recorded a 231 mph (372 km/h) gust, which stood as the world record for a surface wind until Olivia.

3. **A derecho is a long-lived windstorm that moves in a straight line.** The word is Spanish for "straight." Gustavus Hinrichs, a University of Iowa professor, proposed it in 1888. NOAA's Storm Prediction Center describes one as a swath of damage at least 240 miles long with gusts of 58 mph or more.

4. **Iowa got a derecho in August 2020.** On 10 August a line of storms crossed the state and parts of the Midwest, with wind gusts of more than 100 mph in places. Damage was estimated at over $10 billion, one of the costliest thunderstorm events in U.S. history.

5. **A microburst is a column of sinking air that hits the ground and spreads out.** Ted Fujita identified them after the crash of Eastern Air Lines Flight 66 at New York's JFK Airport in 1975, which killed 112 people. His analysis showed that the plane had flown into a downdraft.

6. **Another microburst crash led to better detection.** Delta Flight 191 hit the ground near Dallas–Fort Worth in 1985 and 137 people died. The accident was a major push behind Doppler weather radar at airports.

7. **Supercells got their name in 1962.** Keith Browning and Frank Ludlam described a storm that hit near Wokingham, England, with a persistent rotating updraft, and called it a "supercell."

8. **Storm Eunice set England's gust record in 2022.** On 18 February, a gust of 122 mph (196 km/h) was recorded at The Needles on the Isle of Wight, the strongest ever measured in England.

9. **A "sting jet" is a narrow stream of very fast air inside a storm.** Browning proposed it in 2004 after studying the Great Storm of October 1987 in Britain, which toppled an estimated 15 million trees. Some scientists regard its role in that storm as still debated.

10. **Storm Ciara pushed a Boeing 747 to 825 mph.** On 8 February 2020, a British Airways flight from New York to London rode a jet stream of roughly 260 mph and arrived in 4 hours 56 minutes, a record for a subsonic airliner. The airspeed stayed below the speed of sound.

11. **A bomb cyclone is a real technical term.** Fred Sanders and John Gyakum at MIT coined "bomb" in 1980 for a mid-latitude storm whose pressure falls at least 24 millibars in 24 hours, scaled for latitude.

12. **Cape Denison in Antarctica is among the windiest places at sea level.** Douglas Mawson's 1911–14 expedition camped there and titled his book *The Home of the Blizzard*. Cold, dense air pours off the ice sheet nearly continuously.

13. **The Bora is a winter wind that hits the Adriatic coast.** Cold air spills over the Dinaric Alps and rushes down toward the sea, sometimes producing gusts of 150 km/h or more. Bridges and roads in Croatia close when it blows.

14. **The Santa Ana winds feed California's worst fires.** Dry air from the interior flows downhill toward the coast and warms as it goes, with gusts that can top 70 mph in the mountain passes.

15. **The mistral blows down the Rhône valley into southern France.** It's cold, dry, and can last for days. Vincent van Gogh complained about it while painting in Arles.

16. **Downslope winds heat air as they descend.** The chinook in the Rockies, the foehn in the Alps, and the Santa Ana are all examples. Air warms by compression as it sinks, which is how Loma, Montana, got a 103-degree swing in a day (see Chapter 16).

17. **A wind turbine can be shut down by too much of it.** Most large turbines stop when the wind exceeds about 25 metres per second, around 56 mph, to prevent damage.

18. **The cup anemometer dates to 1846.** John Thomas Romney Robinson of Armagh Observatory built the first one, with four hemispherical cups on a spinning arm. He assumed the cups turned at one-third of the wind's speed, and later experiments showed the true ratio was closer to one-half, so early readings ran high.

19. **Wind speeds are reported in different ways.** A sustained wind is an average over a set time, usually one or ten minutes. A gust is a brief peak, normally lasting a few seconds. The distinction explains why different agencies give different numbers for the same storm.

20. **A Fujita-scale rating is based on damage, not measurements.** Because tornadoes seldom hit an instrument, surveyors estimate wind from how buildings and trees failed.
