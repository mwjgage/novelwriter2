# Chapter 16: Scorching, Freezing, and Everything in Between

Temperature records come with footnotes. The thermometer matters, the site matters, and sometimes the argument runs a hundred years. These are the numbers the WMO keeps on file, plus a few things that happen when it gets too hot or too cold.

1. **The hottest air temperature on record is 56.7°C (134.1°F).** It was measured at Furnace Creek, Death Valley, on 10 July 1913. Some researchers have questioned the reading, citing possible observer error, so the record is **[DISPUTED]**, even though it's the WMO's official figure.

2. **The most reliable modern reading is 54.4°C (129.9°F).** Death Valley reached that on 16 August 2020, and again in July 2021. Many climatologists treat those as the highest well-documented temperatures.

3. **The coldest air temperature on record is −89.2°C (−128.6°F).** It was recorded at Vostok Station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983.

4. **Satellites have seen colder.** Instruments on orbiting satellites measured surface temperatures near −93°C on the East Antarctic Plateau in 2010. That's a ground-surface reading, not an air temperature, so it isn't counted in the WMO record.

5. **Oymyakon and Verkhoyansk dispute the title of coldest inhabited place.** Siberian readings of about −67°C in the early twentieth century and 1892 respectively are cited for each, and the numbers are contested.

6. **Canada hit 49.6°C in June 2021.** The temperature in Lytton, British Columbia, on 29 June broke the national record by almost five degrees. The next day a wildfire destroyed most of the village.

7. **Britain topped 40°C for the first time on 19 July 2022.** Coningsby, Lincolnshire, recorded 40.3°C. Runways at Luton Airport were damaged by the heat, and rail speeds were cut across the country.

8. **Siberia reached 38°C inside the Arctic Circle.** The reading at Verkhoyansk on 20 June 2020 was later verified by the WMO as the highest temperature recorded north of the Arctic Circle.

9. **Loma, Montana, saw a 103-degree swing in 24 hours.** On 14 and 15 January 1972, the temperature rose from −54°F to 49°F as a chinook wind arrived, the largest 24-hour change ever recorded.

10. **The 2003 European heat wave killed tens of thousands.** A study estimated about 70,000 excess deaths across Europe that summer, with France among the hardest hit.

11. **Texas froze in February 2021.** Winter Storm Uri knocked out power to more than 4 million customers. Hundreds died, and burst pipes flooded homes.

12. **Iguanas fall out of trees in cold snaps.** Florida's iguanas are cold-blooded and go into a stunned state when it drops below about 5°C. Photos of them lying on the ground have been published in 2010, 2018, and 2021, and most revive when they warm up.

13. **Dallol, Ethiopia, has the highest annual average temperature of any inhabited place.** The yearly mean is about 34°C. The area's salt flats and acid pools look like another planet.

14. **Wind chill was invented in Antarctica.** Paul Siple and Charles Passel developed the concept in 1939 during research on how fast water freezes in cold wind. The U.S. formula was revised in 2001.

15. **Frying an egg on the sidewalk is mostly a [MYTH].** Eggs set at around 62 to 70°C. A sunny pavement can reach 60°C or more, but it conducts heat poorly and the results are usually unappetising.

16. **Heat can bend railways.** Steel rails expand and can buckle, called "sun kinks." Railways in Britain and the U.S. apply speed limits during extreme heat.

17. **Heat waves are the leading weather killer in many countries.** In the United States, extreme heat has tended to cause more deaths per year than hurricanes, tornadoes, or floods.

18. **The "wet-bulb" temperature measures how well sweat cools you.** Researchers say a sustained wet-bulb temperature of about 35°C would be unsurvivable for healthy people for long. That's a **[THEORY]** based on physiology models, and real-world limits may be lower.

19. **Sound carries farther on cold, still nights.** Under a temperature inversion, with cold air near the ground and warmer air above, sound waves bend back down, so distant trains and traffic seem close.

20. **Frost fairs once took place on the Thames.** In cold winters from the 1600s to 1814, the river in London froze hard enough to hold markets. The period is often tied to the Little Ice Age, and the old London Bridge, whose narrow arches slowed the water, is often cited as a contributing factor.
