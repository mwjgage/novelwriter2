# Chapter 9: When It Rains Fish, Frogs, and Red Water

People have been reporting animals falling out of the sky for at least two thousand years. Some of these stories are legend. A few are documented well enough that scientists take them seriously. This chapter tries to keep the two apart.

1. **Pliny the Elder catalogued strange rains.** His *Natural History*, finished around 77 CE, lists showers of milk, blood, flesh, iron, and wool among the portents reported in the Roman world. They're secondhand accounts and can't be checked.

2. **Athenaeus quoted older writers on a fish rain.** In the *Deipnosophistae*, written around 200 CE, he cites earlier authors describing fish that fell on parts of the Balkans, with stories of days of fish. It's among the oldest written reports of animals falling from the sky.

3. **A U.S. biologist saw it happen in 1947.** On 23 October 1947, in Marksville, Louisiana, A.D. Bajkov of the state's Department of Wildlife and Fisheries reported fish dropping onto the town during a morning of light rain. He published an account in *Science* in 1949.

4. **The usual explanation is a waterspout.** The idea is that a strong updraft over water could lift small fish or frogs and drop them miles away. It's a **[THEORY]**: reports of fish rains fit it, but nobody has directly watched a waterspout carry animals.

5. **Fish rains tend to feature one species.** Many accounts say all the fallen animals were the same kind and about the same size. That pattern is what you would expect from a vortex that sorts by weight, and it's one reason the waterspout explanation has lasted.

6. **Lajamanu, Australia, has had fish rain.** In February 2010, residents of this Northern Territory community reported hundreds of small spangled perch falling after a storm, in an area far from open water. Locals said it had also happened in 2004.

7. **In Yoro, Honduras, people say fish fall every year.** The *Lluvia de Peces* festival marks an event residents describe as happening between May and July after heavy storms. Scientists haven't confirmed the cause, and the story rests largely on local testimony.

8. **Frog rains turn up in newspaper archives.** Reports of frogs falling during storms appear in many countries and centuries. Few were examined by scientists at the time, so most remain **[UNCONFIRMED]**.

9. **In 1876, meat fell in Kentucky.** Chunks of a fleshy substance fell near Olympian Springs, Bath County, from a clear sky. Examiners at the time described them as lung tissue and cartilage. One later suggestion is that vultures vomited as they flew, which remains a **[THEORY]**.

10. **Red rain fell in Kerala in 2001.** After a loud sound heard in the area, red-coloured rain fell for several weeks in parts of southern India. Physicist Godfrey Louis proposed that the particles were extraterrestrial cells, which is a **[THEORY]** that mainstream scientists reject. An Indian government study identified them as spores of a common terrestrial algae, *Trentepohlia*.

11. **Saharan dust can colour rain red.** In Europe, storms that mix with dust from North Africa sometimes leave cars and windows coated in orange-red mud. It's often called "blood rain" in the press.

12. **Raindrops aren't teardrop-shaped.** A falling drop flattens at the bottom into a shape closer to a hamburger bun. Drops larger than about 6 millimetres break up before they reach the ground.

13. **The smell of rain has a name.** In 1964, two Australian scientists, Isabel Bear and Richard Thomas, called it petrichor in a paper in *Nature*. Humans can detect geosmin, one of the compounds responsible, at a few parts per trillion.

14. **High-speed cameras caught rain releasing the smell.** In 2015, MIT researchers filmed raindrops hitting porous surfaces and showed that tiny bubbles in the drop burst upward and shoot aerosols into the air, carrying scents from the soil.

15. **Virga is rain that never lands.** The streaks hanging under a cloud are precipitation evaporating in dry air before it reaches the ground. In deserts, a storm can look like it's pouring over a landscape that stays bone dry.

16. **Hail has fallen out of cloudless skies in some reports.** Ice chunks the size of fists have been reported falling from clear weather, including in Spain in 2000. Researchers called them megacryometeors, and their origin is still unexplained, with ideas ranging from aircraft ice to unusual atmospheric processes. Treat it as **[UNCONFIRMED]**.

17. **Snow can be red and pink.** Alpine and polar snow sometimes turns red, orange, or pink because of algae living in it. The "watermelon snow" in Chapter 5 is the best-known example.

18. **Rain on the equator may be more reliable than anywhere else.** The Intertropical Convergence Zone, a belt of rising air near the equator, brings near-daily storms in some regions. Its position shifts north and south through the year, and the monsoon follows.

19. **"Raining cats and dogs" is not about animals falling.** The origin of the expression is unclear, with several proposed explanations and no firm evidence for any of them. Treat all of them as **[THEORY]**.

20. **Ordinary rain is slightly acidic.** Carbon dioxide dissolving in raindrops gives clean rain a pH of around 5.6. The term "acid rain" was coined in 1872 by the Scottish chemist Robert Angus Smith, after he measured polluted rain in industrial Manchester.
