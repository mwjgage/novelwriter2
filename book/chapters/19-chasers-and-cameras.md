# Chapter 19: The Daredevils and Gadgets That Caught It All

Weather footage got better because someone built a more expensive, more sensitive, or more reckless camera. Here's some of the equipment, and some of the people behind it.

1. **The first weather satellite launched on 1 April 1960.** NASA's TIROS-1 sent back television pictures of cloud patterns, and for the first time forecasters could see storms from above.

2. **Satellites now watch continuously.** NOAA's GOES-16, launched in 2016, delivers imagery of the Western Hemisphere every few minutes and carries a lightning mapper. Japan's Himawari-8, launched in 2014, scans the full disc of Earth every 10 minutes.

3. **The first hook echo was spotted on radar in 1953.** On 9 April 1953, scientists at the Illinois State Water Survey noticed a hook shape on radar associated with a tornado near Champaign. It became a classic signature of a tornadic supercell.

4. **Hurricane Hunters have flown since 1943.** After Colonel Joseph Duckworth's first flight, the U.S. Air Force and NOAA developed regular reconnaissance missions. Today's crews fly WC-130J aircraft and NOAA P-3 Orions through storms.

5. **Dropsondes measure the inside of a hurricane.** Aircraft release small instrument packages on parachutes that report wind, pressure, temperature, and humidity as they fall to the sea.

6. **Oklahoma City's KWTV was early to buy Doppler radar.** In 1981, the station became the first commercial broadcaster to install a Doppler weather radar. Chief meteorologist Gary England used it on air for decades.

7. **The national Doppler network came in the 1990s.** The WSR-88D (NEXRAD) system comprises roughly 160 radars across the United States. It replaced older equipment and improved tornado warning times.

8. **Doppler on Wheels is a radar on a truck.** Josh Wurman started the program in 1995. One of its trucks measured the 301 mph wind in the 1999 Moore tornado, and the fleet took part in the VORTEX2 project.

9. **TOTO was a barrel with sensors.** The TOtable Tornado Observatory was developed in the 1980s to be placed in a tornado's path. It never got a direct hit, though the movie *Twister* (1996) borrowed the idea for its own sensor package.

10. **The word "tornado" was once banned from forecasts.** In the late 1880s the Army Signal Service stopped using it, fearing panic, and the ban lasted for decades. On 25 March 1948, Air Force forecasters Ernest Fawbush and Robert Miller at Tinker Air Force Base issued a tornado forecast that verified, and it helped change official attitudes.

11. **David Hoadley has chased since 1956.** He's often called a pioneer of modern storm chasing and founded the magazine *Storm Track* in 1977, which connected chasers and researchers.

12. **SKYWARN trained spotters across the country.** The National Weather Service started the program in the 1960s. Hundreds of thousands of volunteers now report tornadoes, hail, and floods.

13. **The Tornado Intercept Vehicle is an armoured truck for filming.** Filmmaker Sean Casey built the first one in the early 2000s to put IMAX cameras close to tornadoes. Its successor has hydraulic spikes that anchor it to the ground, and it was featured in the 2011 film *Tornado Alley*.

14. **Doorbell cameras and dashcams changed the footage.** Many of the recent famous clips of tornadoes, hail, and floods were shot by ordinary devices left running by their owners. Researchers now collect them as data.

15. **Smartphones have barometers.** Some phones record air pressure, and researchers have explored collecting the readings from thousands of devices to improve short-term forecasts.

16. **The public can report weather by phone.** The National Severe Storms Laboratory's mPING app, launched in 2012, lets anyone report hail, rain type, or ice, and the reports feed research and radar verification.

17. **The Enhanced Fujita scale started in 2007.** It replaced the original F-scale with a damage-based system using 28 types of damage indicators, from houses to trees.

18. **A TV crew's SUV was thrown by the El Reno tornado.** On 31 May 2013, a vehicle carrying a Weather Channel crew was lifted and tossed by the storm. They survived with injuries, and the day, which also killed three experienced chasers, led to widespread discussion of chaser safety.

19. **High-speed cameras show lightning in slow motion.** Phantom cameras record at hundreds of thousands of frames per second. Footage of leader channels branching toward the ground has helped researchers study how a strike chooses its path.

20. **Time-lapse made supercells famous.** Photographers such as Mike Olbinski have produced time-lapse films of Plains storms in the 2010s that millions have watched, helping the public see storm structure.
