# Chapter 1: Clouds That Look Like Alien Invasions

Clouds cover about two-thirds of the planet at any moment, and most of them behave. These ones don't. Some look like stormy oceans hung upside down, some punch holes in themselves, and one rolls across northern Australia at highway speed while glider pilots ride it for fun.

1. **A London pharmacist named every cloud.** In an 1802 lecture, Luke Howard proposed Latin names for cloud types: cumulus, stratus, cirrus, and combinations of them. His essay followed in 1803, and the names stuck. The German poet Goethe admired the system enough to write poems about Howard.

2. **A fair-weather cumulus weighs about as much as a hundred elephants.** NCAR scientist Peggy LeMone's estimate for a cloud roughly a kilometre across comes to around 500 metric tonnes of water droplets, or 1.1 million pounds. It stays up because the droplets are tiny and the air beneath is rising.

3. **The 2017 cloud atlas added a long list of new names.** The World Meteorological Organization's updated International Cloud Atlas included asperitas, volutus (roll cloud), fluctus (wave cloud), cavum (hole), murus (wall cloud), and cauda (tail cloud), among others.

4. **Some clouds are named for what made them.** The 2017 atlas also lists flammagenitus (born from fire), silvagenitus (born from forests), cataractagenitus (born from waterfalls), and homogenitus (born from human activity, such as the trails behind aircraft).

5. **Asperitas looks like a rough sea seen from below.** Its undulating, dark base prompted the Cloud Appreciation Society, founded in 2004 by Gavin Pretor-Pinney, to campaign for it to be recognised. The WMO added it in 2017. Despite its drama it doesn't usually bring severe weather, and meteorologists are still working out exactly how it forms.

6. **Kelvin-Helmholtz clouds are breaking waves in the sky.** They form where two layers of air slide past each other at different speeds, and they're named for the physicists Lord Kelvin and Hermann von Helmholtz. The curls often last only a minute or two before they smear out.

7. **The same instability shows up on other worlds.** Kelvin-Helmholtz waves have been observed along Saturn's magnetosphere and in the Sun's corona, as well as in Earth's own clouds.

8. **Lenticular clouds look like flying saucers, and have been reported as UFOs.** They form in the crest of a wave of air flowing over a mountain, which is why they hover above peaks like Mount Fuji and Mount Shasta without moving.

9. **That same mountain wave carried a glider to the stratosphere.** In 2006, Steve Fossett and Einar Enevoldson set a glider altitude record of 15,447 metres (50,671 feet) riding mountain wave over Argentina during the Perlan Project. The Perlan 2 glider later passed 76,000 feet in 2018.

10. **Mammatus clouds hang in pouches.** These bulges form on the underside of storm anvils where cooler, moister air sinks. They're associated with severe thunderstorms but they don't cause tornadoes, and they often show up after the worst has passed.

11. **Hole-punch clouds have a cause scientists pinned down in 2011.** A study in *Science* by Andrew Heymsfield and colleagues showed fallstreak holes form when aircraft pass through a layer of supercooled water droplets. The droplets, below freezing and still liquid, abruptly freeze around the disturbance and fall out as ice, leaving a hole.

12. **The hole often comes with a streak of ice crystals beneath it.** That streak is the ice that fell out of the cloud, evaporating before it reaches the ground. At sunset the whole thing can glow, and it has been mistaken for a UFO more than once.

13. **The Morning Glory is a cloud that rolls.** Near Burketown on the Gulf of Carpentaria in Queensland, long tubes of cloud can stretch across the sky, sometimes a few hundred kilometres long, and travel at speeds up to roughly 60 km/h. Glider pilots go there to surf the lifting air at their leading edge.

14. **Nacreous clouds glow in pastel colours.** Also called polar stratospheric clouds, they form at around 15 to 25 kilometres up, in extremely cold air in polar winter. Their pearly colours come from tiny ice particles diffracting sunlight, and they play a part in ozone destruction.

15. **The highest clouds on Earth shine after dark.** Noctilucent clouds sit about 76 to 85 kilometres up, high enough that the Sun still lights them when the ground below is dark. They were first documented in 1885. A popular idea says they appeared after the 1883 Krakatoa eruption, but that link remains **[THEORY]**.

16. **Shelf clouds look like the lid of a spaceship.** Also called arcus clouds, they form at the leading edge of outflow from a thunderstorm, when cool air pushes under warm, moist air and lifts it.

17. **Pileus clouds are caps on top of clouds.** A thin smooth cloud may form over a rising tower of cumulus as the tower pushes the air above it upward and cools it past the dew point. The cap can vanish as the tower punches through.

18. **Banner clouds trail from mountain summits.** On the downwind side of sharp peaks like the Matterhorn, air circles back and cools, forming a plume that looks like a flag attached to the summit even on an otherwise clear day.

19. **Clouds can make patterns that look like a river's wake.** Satellite images from Landsat show rows of alternating vortices, called Kármán vortex streets, streaming downwind of mountainous islands such as Chile's Juan Fernández Islands. The islands are small and the swirls are tens of kilometres across.

20. **A single lab session started cloud seeding.** On 13 November 1946, Vincent Schaefer of General Electric dropped dry ice into a cloud from a plane over Massachusetts and watched snow begin to fall. The experiment launched decades of weather modification attempts, whose effectiveness is still debated.

21. **Fog is a cloud that touches the ground, and some creatures drink it.** In the Namib Desert, darkling beetles of the genus *Stenocara* collect fog droplets on their backs and let the water run to their mouths. People now build mesh nets in places like Chile's Atacama to harvest fog the same way.
