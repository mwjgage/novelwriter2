# Chapter 3: Tornado Tales and Twisting Terrors

Tornadoes are the smallest storms in this book and some of the hardest to measure. They last minutes, they destroy the instruments you point at them, and they've been caught on camera for 140 years. The footage taught researchers most of what they know.

1. **The widest tornado on record was 2.6 miles across.** The El Reno, Oklahoma tornado of 31 May 2013 spanned about 4.2 kilometres at its peak. It was rated EF3 based on damage, though radar measured winds in subvortices near 300 mph.

2. **The Tri-State Tornado (1925) stayed on the ground for 219 miles.** It moved at roughly 62 mph across Missouri, Illinois, and Indiana on 18 March 1925, killing 695 people. No continuous tornado track since has beaten it.

3. **The fastest tornado wind ever measured was 301 mph.** A Doppler on Wheels radar led by Joshua Wurman recorded it in the Bridge Creek–Moore tornado on 3 May 1999. The estimate carries an uncertainty of about 20 mph.

4. **April 1974 produced 148 tornadoes in under two days.** The Super Outbreak stretched across 13 states on 3 and 4 April and killed over 300 people. Thirty of its tornadoes were rated F4 or F5.

5. **The April 2011 outbreak produced over 360 tornadoes.** Across 25 to 28 April, the storms killed around 320 people, mostly in Alabama, and it remains the largest tornado outbreak in recorded history.

6. **The first tornado photographs date from 1884.** Two are among the oldest known: one from Kansas on 26 April 1884, near Garnett, and one from Howard, South Dakota on 28 August the same year. Exposure times were short enough, barely, to catch the funnels.

7. **The Fujita scale came from a man who also discovered downbursts.** Ted Fujita of the University of Chicago devised the F-scale in 1971. His later work identified microbursts, which changed how airports manage wind shear.

8. **No tornado has been rated EF5 since 2013.** The Enhanced Fujita scale replaced the original in 2007. The last EF5 was the Moore, Oklahoma tornado of 20 May 2013, and as of this writing none has been rated since.

9. **Hallam, Nebraska held the width record before El Reno.** The EF4 that struck on 22 May 2004 was about 2.5 miles wide at its largest. Its width was measured by survey, not radar.

10. **A probe once measured a 100-millibar pressure drop inside a tornado.** In June 2003, Tim Samaras placed a pressure probe in the path of a tornado near Manchester, South Dakota. It recorded the largest drop ever measured in a tornado, about 100 millibars.

11. **Tornadoes cross rivers.** The idea that rivers or hills protect a town is a **[MYTH]**. The Tri-State Tornado crossed the Mississippi, and tornadoes have run over hills, valleys, and downtown districts.

12. **Opening the windows to equalise pressure doesn't help.** That advice used to circulate widely and is a **[MYTH]**. Houses are damaged by wind and flying debris, not by pressure differences, and the time wasted could be spent getting to shelter.

13. **The deadliest tornado in history hit Bangladesh.** The Daulatpur–Saturia tornado of 26 April 1989 is estimated to have killed around 1,300 people. Dense population and weak housing contributed to the toll.

14. **The UK has more tornadoes per square mile than almost anywhere.** It records an average of about 30 to 50 a year, most of them weak. The United States, by comparison, sees roughly 1,200 annually.

15. **Waterspouts are common in the Florida Keys.** Fair-weather waterspouts there number in the hundreds each year, mostly weak, and form over warm shallow water under developing cumulus clouds.

16. **Some tornadoes contain several smaller vortices.** Multi-vortex tornadoes have suction vortices that spin around the main funnel like horses on a carousel. They explain why one house can be destroyed while its neighbour stands.

17. **Landspouts form without a thunderstorm's rotating updraft.** They grow from the ground up, when a rotating column of air under a growing cumulus is stretched upward. They look like tornadoes and are usually weaker.

18. **Gustnadoes are swirls of dust along a storm's gust front.** They look tornadic and sometimes have a funnel, but they aren't connected to a rotating cloud base above them.

19. **Tornadoes are most common in the United States.** The central plains see the clash of warm, moist Gulf air with dry air from the Rockies and cold air from Canada. Dixie Alley, across the Southeast, has fewer storms but more nighttime tornadoes and a higher death rate.

20. **A famous story says tornadoes can pluck chickens.** There are many reports across the twentieth century of birds found stripped of feathers after storms. Researchers have proposed that the force of wind and debris could do it, but individual cases are mostly anecdotal and **[UNCONFIRMED]**.

21. **Storm chasers have studied tornadoes since the 1970s.** The VORTEX2 project in 2009 and 2010 was the largest tornado research effort ever attempted, with dozens of vehicles and instruments deployed across the Plains.

22. **Tim Samaras died chasing.** Samaras, his son Paul, and Carl Young were killed at El Reno in 2013 when the tornado changed direction and size. The deaths are widely described as the first of storm chasers killed by a tornado, and they changed how many research teams plan their routes.
