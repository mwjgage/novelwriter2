# Chapter 4: Fire Tornadoes and Sky-High Infernos

Large fires make their own weather. They build clouds, pull in wind, and sometimes spin up vortices that rise a kilometre or more. Some of the events here caused terrible losses, and the facts are told plainly for that reason.

1. **The Carr Fire spawned a fire tornado rated EF3.** On 26 July 2018, near Redding, California, a fire whirl formed with winds that the National Weather Service estimated at over 140 mph. The Weather Service rated it after surveying the damage, an unusual step for a vortex that fire created.

2. **Canberra's 2003 firestorm made a confirmed fire tornado.** On 18 January 2003, a fire-induced vortex was identified by Australia's Bureau of Meteorology and rated F2. The fires killed four people and destroyed about 500 homes.

3. **A fire whirl killed roughly 38,000 people in 1923.** After the Great Kanto earthquake, fires swept Tokyo, and a firestorm swept through the Army Clothing Depot, where tens of thousands had taken shelter. It is one of the deadliest fire whirls in history.

4. **The Peshtigo Fire of 1871 killed more people than any other U.S. wildfire.** It burned across northeastern Wisconsin on 8 October, the same night as the Great Chicago Fire, and estimates put the death toll between 1,200 and 2,500. Survivors described winds and a "tornado of fire."

5. **Hamburg's 1943 firestorm pulled winds of hurricane force into the city.** The bombing raids of July 1943 created a firestorm that drew in air from all sides. Weather researchers have studied it since as an example of fire-driven wind.

6. **Pyrocumulonimbus clouds are thunderstorms built by fire.** Heat and smoke from a large fire rise into the atmosphere, pull in moisture, and form storm clouds that produce lightning, hail, and sometimes tornadoes. They're abbreviated pyroCb.

7. **An Australian smoke plume rose to the stratosphere.** After the Black Summer fires of 2019–20, a rotating smoke vortex reached about 35 kilometres (22 miles) up. Satellite instruments tracked the smoke around the Southern Hemisphere.

8. **Smoke can rise by heating itself.** Canadian wildfire smoke in 2017 reached the stratosphere and then climbed higher, absorbing sunlight and rising to around 23 kilometres, according to a 2019 study.

9. **PyroCb lightning can start new fires.** Lightning from fire-generated storms has ignited fresh blazes ahead of the original fire front, a hazard for firefighters because it spreads the fire beyond containment lines.

10. **Fire-born clouds got their own official name.** The 2017 International Cloud Atlas lists "flammagenitus" for clouds produced by fire, with pyrocumulonimbus as the extreme case.

11. **A blue whirl was discovered in a lab in 2016.** University of Maryland researchers burning fuel on water inside a fire-whirl chamber found that it could shift into a small, stable blue flame that burns very cleanly. They published it in *PNAS*.

12. **Fire whirls can occur in oil-spill burns.** The blue whirl experiment was done partly to study whether similar structures might burn spilled oil more efficiently. That use remains a research idea.

13. **Fire whirls come in all sizes.** Dust-devil-scale fire devils form on small grass fires all the time. The big ones, hundreds of metres tall and spinning for many minutes, are much rarer.

14. **Flames themselves can reach over 1,000°C.** Wildfire flame temperatures typically range from about 800 to 1,200°C, hot enough to melt aluminium. That's the temperature of the flame, not necessarily the heat felt downwind.

15. **A firefighter once saved himself by lighting another fire.** In the 1949 Mann Gulch fire in Montana, smokejumper foreman Wag Dodge lit an "escape fire" in the grass ahead of him and lay down in the burnt ground. He survived. Thirteen men died.

16. **New York's sky turned orange in 2023.** Smoke from Canadian wildfires drifted south, and on 7 June 2023 the city's air quality index passed 400 at times, among the worst measured in a major city that day.

17. **The smoke crossed the Atlantic.** Particles from those same Canadian fires were detected in Europe later in June 2023, including in Norway.

18. **California's sky went dark at noon in 2020.** On 9 September 2020, smoke over the San Francisco Bay Area turned daylight a deep orange, an event widely photographed and nicknamed Orange Day.

19. **Wildfires can reach cloud heights comparable to volcano eruptions.** The largest pyroCb events send material into the lower stratosphere, which is the same region large volcanic eruptions reach.

20. **Satellites see fires from orbit.** NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments detect thermal hot spots, and the International Space Station crew regularly photographs smoke plumes.
