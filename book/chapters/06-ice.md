# Chapter 6: Ice That Grows Hair and Other Frozen Weirdness

Ice does things you wouldn't expect a solid to do. It sprouts white filaments, makes spinning discs, sings, and builds spires underwater. These facts come from lakes, rivers, roads, and the polar seas.

1. **Hair ice grows on dead branches, and a fungus is involved.** Thin silky strands, each a fraction of a millimetre across, extrude from the wood on cold, humid nights. In 2015, researchers Gerhard Wagner and Christian Mätzler identified the fungus *Exidiopsis effusa* as a key factor. Alfred Wegener, better known for continental drift, had proposed a biological cause in 1918.

2. **Hair ice keeps its shape for hours.** The fungus appears to slow the recrystallisation that would normally turn the fine hairs into lumps of ice. Remove the fungus from a branch and the effect doesn't appear.

3. **Frost flowers bloom on new sea ice.** On very cold, calm nights in polar regions, salty brine pushes up through a thin ice layer and freezes into feathery clusters. They're salt-rich and can host bacteria.

4. **Brinicles are underwater icicles that freeze everything near them.** Extremely cold, salty brine sinking from sea ice forms a tube that grows toward the seabed. A BBC film crew captured one in the Antarctic for *Frozen Planet* in 2011, and the brinicle froze starfish on the seabed in its path.

5. **Ice circles rotate on rivers.** In January 2019, a disc about 100 metres across was filmed turning slowly on the Presumpscot River in Westbrook, Maine. It made roughly one rotation every 30 minutes. Researchers think eddies in the current shear the edges of a floating chunk and keep it spinning.

6. **Ice volcanoes erupt without heat.** On the shore of Lake Michigan and other big lakes, waves surge through cracks in a shelf of ice and spray water upward. It freezes in a cone as it falls.

7. **Ice can shove itself ashore.** In Alaska, the Inuit word *ivu* refers to ice pushed up onto the land by wind or current. Piles can reach several metres high and have crushed buildings.

8. **Ice eggs wash up on beaches.** In November 2019, thousands of ice balls, some the size of footballs, covered a beach on Hailuoto island in Finland. They form when small pieces of slush tumble in the surf and gather layers of ice as they roll.

9. **Lake Baikal holds about 20 percent of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water.** It's also the deepest lake on Earth at around 1,640 metres. In winter its ice thickens enough for trucks to drive across, and the surface cracks with sounds like gunfire.

10. **Frozen methane bubbles stack like layered coins.** In some Baikal and Canadian lakes, bubbles rising from decaying matter below freeze in columns under clear ice. The bubbles look like white pancakes suspended in glass.

11. **Pancake ice is real and looks like it sounds.** In choppy polar seas, new ice forms round plates with raised rims because the plates bump into each other. They can be anywhere from a few centimetres to several metres across.

12. **Niagara Falls once stopped running.** On 29 March 1848, an ice jam at the eastern end of Lake Erie blocked the Niagara River upstream of the falls. For roughly 30 hours the flow slowed to a trickle, and people walked on the exposed riverbed.

13. **The 1998 North American ice storm brought down the power grid.** In January 1998, freezing rain coated Quebec, eastern Ontario, and parts of New York and New England. More than a million households in Quebec lost electricity, some for weeks, and steel transmission towers folded.

14. **Ice can sing.** Thermal contraction on a frozen lake sends waves through the ice that make laser-like zaps when you skip a stone or skate across. Recordings of it have been used in sound design for sci-fi films.

15. **The ground can explode in the cold.** A frost quake, or cryoseism, happens when water in soil freezes suddenly and the ground cracks with a boom. They hit parts of the U.S. Midwest during the January 2014 polar vortex.

16. **A glacier calving event was filmed in 2008.** On 28 May 2008 at Ilulissat Glacier in Greenland, photographers from the Extreme Ice Survey recorded what was then the largest calving event ever filmed. The ice face was about three miles wide and rose several hundred feet.

17. **An ice shelf the size of Rhode Island broke apart in weeks.** The Larsen B shelf in Antarctica shed about 3,250 square kilometres between late January and early March 2002. Satellites watched the pieces separate.

18. **Mercury has ice.** In 2012, NASA's MESSENGER probe found evidence of water ice in permanently shadowed craters near the planet's poles, despite daytime temperatures hot enough to melt lead.

19. **Ice can burn.** Methane hydrate, sometimes called fire ice, is a cage of water molecules trapped around methane. Hold a flame to a chunk and the gas ignites while the ice melts.

20. **Soap bubbles freeze into crystal globes.** At around −10°C to −15°C, a bubble blown outdoors can freeze in seconds, with crystals spreading across its surface. Frozen bubbles shatter easily and often collapse when they land.

21. **Snowball Earth is a hypothesis.** Some geologists argue that the planet was nearly or completely covered in ice about 700 million years ago. The idea is **[THEORY]**, still debated, particularly the question of whether the oceans were frozen all the way to the equator.

22. **The Mpemba effect says hot water can freeze faster than cold.** It's named after Erasto Mpemba, a Tanzanian student who noticed it in the 1960s. Experiments have given mixed results, and it remains **[DEBATED]**.
