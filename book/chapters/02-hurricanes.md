# Chapter 2: Hurricanes With Eyeballs

A tropical cyclone is a heat engine the size of a small country. From a satellite it looks like a pinwheel with a hole in the middle, and the hole is the interesting part. These facts come from aircraft that flew into storms on purpose, buoys that rode them out, and cameras that kept rolling anyway.

1. **Hurricane Patricia (2015) had the strongest winds ever measured in a tropical cyclone.** The National Hurricane Center's post-season analysis put its peak at 215 mph (345 km/h) with a central pressure of 872 millibars, the lowest ever in the Western Hemisphere. That ties it with Typhoon Nancy of 1961 for the top wind speed on record.

2. **Typhoon Tip (1979) holds the lowest pressure ever recorded, at 870 millibars.** It was also the largest tropical cyclone on record, with a diameter of about 2,220 kilometres (1,380 miles), wider than the distance from Paris to Moscow.

3. **Patricia gained more than 100 mph of wind in about a day.** It was a tropical storm earlier that week, and its intensification was among the fastest forecasters have recorded. Then it hit a mountainous stretch of Mexico's Pacific coast and weakened quickly, which spared the country from the worst-case outcome.

4. **Eyewalls look like a stadium from inside.** Flight crews describe the "stadium effect": the eyewall curves outward as it rises, like seating tiers, while the clear eye sits at the bottom like a playing field. Some pilots report seeing blue sky overhead.

5. **Hurricane Wilma (2005) had an eye two nautical miles across.** At its smallest, the eye was only about 2.3 miles wide, the smallest NOAA had ever recorded in an Atlantic hurricane. Wilma also hit 882 millibars, the lowest pressure measured in the Atlantic basin.

6. **Eyewalls replace themselves.** In an eyewall replacement cycle, an outer ring of thunderstorms forms around the original eyewall and slowly chokes it. The storm weakens, then often strengthens again with a wider eye. Observers first noticed it in aircraft and microwave satellite data.

7. **Hurricane Dorian (2019) nearly stopped moving over the Bahamas.** At its slowest, its forward speed dropped to about one mile per hour while it sat over Grand Bahama. It came ashore on Elbow Cay in the Abacos with sustained winds of 185 mph, tied for the strongest Atlantic landfall on record.

8. **The first deliberate flight into a hurricane was a bet.** On 27 July 1943, Colonel Joseph Duckworth flew a single-engine AT-6 Texan into a hurricane near Galveston, Texas, after British pilots told him it couldn't be done. He went back later that day with a weather officer, Lieutenant Ralph O'Hair.

9. **NOAA's hurricane planes have nicknames.** Two of its WP-3D Orion aircraft are called Kermit and Miss Piggy. In 1989, one of NOAA's P-3s lost an engine in the eyewall of Hurricane Hugo and the crew brought it home on the other three.

10. **A sailing drone filmed the inside of Hurricane Sam.** In September 2021, a Saildrone robotic vessel steered through the eyewall of Category 4 Hurricane Sam in the Atlantic. It measured waves of about 14 metres (46 feet) and gusts up to 126 mph, and it sent back the first video from the sea surface near a hurricane's eye.

11. **Typhoon Haiyan (2013) hit the Philippines with 195 mph winds.** That is the Joint Typhoon Warning Center's estimate of one-minute sustained speed at peak. Storm surge of several metres swept through Tacloban and the death toll topped 6,000.

12. **Hurricane Katrina's storm surge reached 27.8 feet.** That is the highest surge ever measured in the United States, recorded along the Mississippi coast in 2005. The surge, more than the wind, caused most of the deaths and destruction.

13. **The deepest known storm surge may be from 1899.** Cyclone Mahina struck Bathurst Bay, Australia, and researchers estimate a surge of around 13 metres (42 feet) based on evidence left on the land. It's an estimate from field evidence rather than an instrument reading, and some scientists have questioned the figure.

14. **One storm lasted a month.** Hurricane John in 1994 survived 31 days over the Pacific, covering roughly 13,000 kilometres (8,000 miles) and crossing the International Date Line twice. It remains the longest-lived tropical cyclone on record.

15. **Typhoon Vamei formed almost on the equator.** Tropical cyclones normally can't form within a few degrees of the equator because the Coriolis effect is too weak to start the spin. Vamei did it in December 2001, about 1.5° north of the line, near Singapore, and hit Malaysia.

16. **A hurricane showed up in the South Atlantic.** Many forecasters had considered that impossible until Catarina struck southern Brazil in March 2004, the first recorded hurricane there.

17. **Hurricane Ivan (2004) produced more than 100 tornadoes.** Outer bands of tropical cyclones often spin off short-lived tornadoes, and Ivan set records for the number it made as it moved inland over the southeastern United States.

18. **A hurricane's heat output is about 200 times the world's electrical generating capacity.** That's NOAA's figure for the heat energy a mature storm releases as water vapour condenses. Only a small fraction becomes wind.

19. **Two storms can orbit each other.** The Fujiwhara effect, described by the Japanese meteorologist Sakuhei Fujiwhara in a 1921 paper, happens when two cyclones come close enough to rotate around a shared centre. They can also merge.

20. **The Saffir-Simpson scale grew from an engineer's wind-damage chart.** Herbert Saffir, a structural engineer, made it around 1971, and the National Hurricane Center's director, Robert Simpson, added storm surge. The scale has five categories.

21. **There is no official Category 6.** Some scientists have proposed one for storms over 192 mph, since Patricia and a handful of typhoons have already exceeded that. This remains **[UNOFFICIAL]**, and the National Hurricane Center hasn't adopted it.

22. **Storms used to be named after politicians.** In the 1890s the Australian meteorologist Clement Wragge gave tropical storms the names of politicians he disliked. The United States started using women's names in 1953 and added men's names for Atlantic storms in 1979.

23. **Seabirds get stuck in the eye.** Sailors and aircraft crews have documented flocks of birds circling inside the calm centre, sometimes carried hundreds of kilometres from where they started.
