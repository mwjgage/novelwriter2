# Chapter 12: Rainbows, Moonbows, and Halos That Shouldn't Exist

Light bends, splits, and bounces around inside drops of water and crystals of ice. Almost every strange glow in the sky can be explained by the geometry of that process. Some are still rare enough to make people pull over.

1. **A primary rainbow appears 42 degrees from the point opposite the Sun.** The secondary bow sits near 51 degrees, with its colours reversed. Between the two lies a darker patch of sky.

2. **That dark strip has a name.** It's called Alexander's dark band, after Alexander of Aphrodisias, a Greek philosopher who described it around 200 CE.

3. **Rainbows are full circles.** The ground gets in the way. Passengers in aircraft, and hikers on peaks above the cloud, sometimes see the whole ring.

4. **Extra stripes inside a bow are called supernumerary arcs.** The faint pink-and-green bands appear when light waves from different paths interfere. Thomas Young used them in the early 1800s as evidence that light behaves as a wave.

5. **Twinned rainbows start from one point and split.** The cause is a mix of drop sizes in the rain, and photographs of them are rare.

6. **A moonbow needs a bright full Moon and a dark sky.** They're thousands of times fainter than a rainbow, so the eye sees them as a pale white arc. Cameras with long exposures show colours. Victoria Falls, Yosemite, and Cumberland Falls in Kentucky are known for them.

7. **Fogbows are nearly white.** The droplets in fog are so small, under about 0.05 millimetres, that light diffracts and the colours overlap. Fogbows look like a broad ghostly band.

8. **A rainbow in Taiwan lasted almost nine hours.** Guinness recognises the longest-lasting rainbow as 8 hours and 58 minutes, observed on Yangmingshan, Taipei, on 30 November 2017.

9. **"Double Rainbow Guy" made one go viral.** Paul Vasquez posted a video of a double rainbow near Yosemite in 2010 and it became a meme within weeks.

10. **A circumhorizontal arc looks like a rainbow lying on its side.** Often called a "fire rainbow," it's not a rainbow at all. It forms when sunlight passes through flat, hexagonal ice crystals in high cirrus, and the Sun must be more than about 58 degrees above the horizon.

11. **Sun dogs are bright spots 22 degrees from the Sun.** They form when plate-shaped ice crystals drift with their faces horizontal. They sit on both sides of the Sun, and are called parhelia in the technical literature.

12. **A painting from 1535 records a halo display.** The Vädersolstavlan, or "Sun Dog Painting," in Stockholm's Storkyrkan church depicts an elaborate halo display seen over the city that year. The version on display is a later copy of the original.

13. **A halo around the Sun or Moon is a 22-degree ring.** Hexagonal ice crystals in thin cirrus clouds bend the light. Folklore says a lunar halo means rain is coming, and thin cirrus can be a sign of an approaching front, though the link is unreliable.

14. **Light pillars rise from city lights on cold nights.** Falling plate crystals reflect lamps and headlights into vertical columns. They appear in the Arctic and Canada and are common in photographs of snowy towns.

15. **The green flash lasts a second or two.** At sunset or sunrise over a clear horizon, the atmosphere bends the sun's blue-green light slightly above the red. It's most reliably seen over the sea.

16. **A Brocken spectre is your own shadow.** Named for the Brocken mountain in Germany's Harz range, where Johann Silberschlag described it in 1780, it's your shadow thrown onto cloud below and often surrounded by a rainbow-coloured ring called a glory.

17. **Fata Morgana looks like floating castles.** This layered mirage forms when a temperature inversion bends light along the horizon. It's named after Morgan le Fay and is often reported in the Strait of Messina.

18. **A mirage may have fooled Arctic explorers.** In 1906, Robert Peary reported seeing land to the northwest, which he named Crocker Land. A 1913 expedition went looking and found nothing. Many historians think it was a mirage, though some dispute that, so treat the identification as **[THEORY]**.

19. **The Novaya Zemlya effect makes the Sun appear when it shouldn't.** In 1597, the crew of explorer Willem Barentsz saw the Sun return about two weeks early after the polar night. The cause is strong atmospheric refraction over very cold air.

20. **Sunset is red because blue light scatters away.** Lord Rayleigh worked out the scattering in the 1870s. At low sun, light travels through more air, so the blues scatter out of the beam and the reds stay.

21. **Sun rays seem to fan out, but they're parallel.** Crepuscular rays look like they radiate from the Sun because of perspective, the same reason railway tracks seem to converge. Sometimes they reappear on the opposite horizon as anticrepuscular rays.
