# Chapter 5: Snowflakes, Snow Donuts, and Self-Rolling Snowballs

Snow is frozen water doing its best impression of lace. Under the right conditions it also rolls itself into logs, glows pink with algae, and falls on deserts. Here are twenty-two reasons to take a second look at the stuff.

1. **The first photograph of a snow crystal was taken in 1885.** A Vermont farmer named Wilson Bentley attached a bellows camera to a microscope on 15 January 1885 and got a clear picture. Over his life he photographed more than 5,000 snowflakes and published *Snow Crystals* in 1931. He died that December of pneumonia after walking home in a blizzard.

2. **Two snowflakes have been found that looked the same.** In 1988, Nancy Knight of the National Center for Atmospheric Research collected two simple hexagonal crystals in Wisconsin that appeared identical under the microscope. Complex branching flakes, which have far more ways to differ, almost certainly never repeat.

3. **A Japanese physicist grew the first artificial snow crystals in 1936.** Ukichiro Nakaya at Hokkaido University worked out that a crystal's shape depends on temperature and humidity at the time it grows. He is credited with the line "Snow crystals are letters sent from heaven."

4. **Snowflakes have six sides because water molecules arrange themselves into hexagons.** The hydrogen bonds between molecules lock into a six-fold lattice as ice forms. Dendrites, the classic feathery stars, grow fastest at around −15°C.

5. **Snow rollers form on their own.** When wet snow, a thin icy crust, and a steady wind line up, gusts push a clump across the field. It picks up layers like a rolled carpet. Photographs of fields scattered with them have circulated since the 1800s.

6. **Some snow rollers become donuts.** When the soft centre of a roller blows out or collapses, the log is left hollow. They are rare enough that finding one is a decent excuse to call a neighbour over.

7. **Thundersnow is real and unusually quiet.** Lightning during a snowstorm happens in strongly rising air, often near the Great Lakes or in intense winter cyclones. Snow muffles the thunder, so people close to the strike may hear only a dull boom.

8. **Penitentes are blades of snow, sometimes taller than a person.** Darwin described them in the Andes in 1835, and the name comes from their resemblance to hooded penitents kneeling in rows. They form when strong sunlight at high, dry altitude carves the snow into spikes pointing at the Sun.

9. **Pluto may have its own penitentes.** NASA's New Horizons probe photographed a region of bladed terrain in 2015 on a mountainous area of Pluto. Researchers proposed that the blades are methane ice shaped by the same process, though that is a **[THEORY]** based on modelling and remote imaging.

10. **Watermelon snow is red because of algae.** The species *Chlamydomonas nivalis* produces a red pigment that protects it from strong sunlight. The snow sometimes smells faintly sweet, and the dark colour makes the snow melt faster.

11. **Snow is mostly air.** Fresh snow is typically 90 percent air or more, which is why a foot of the stuff melts down to roughly an inch of water. The exact ratio varies from about 5:1 in wet snow to 20:1 or more in cold, dry powder.

12. **Mount Baker holds the single-season snowfall record.** The ski area in Washington State measured 1,140 inches (95 feet) in the 1998–99 winter, the most ever recorded for a season anywhere.

13. **Silver Lake, Colorado, got 75.8 inches of snow in 24 hours in 1921.** That's the accepted U.S. record for a single day, set on 14–15 April.

14. **An Italian town claims it beat that.** Capracotta reported about 256 centimetres (over 100 inches) in 18 hours in March 2015. The measurement was made by local observers and hasn't been formally verified as a world record, so treat it as **[UNCONFIRMED]**.

15. **The largest snowflake on record may not have been real.** A report from Fort Keogh, Montana, in January 1887 described flakes 15 inches wide and 8 inches thick. It's in record books, but nobody measured it with modern instruments, and many scientists doubt it.

16. **Snow squeaks when it gets cold.** Below roughly −10°C, packed snow underfoot makes a squeaking sound as ice crystals rub together. Warm snow is quieter and more slushy.

17. **Snow has fallen on the Sahara more than once.** Orange dunes in Aïn Séfra, Algeria, were dusted white in December 2016 and again in January 2018. Photos of the contrast spread across the internet within hours.

18. **The Atacama sees snow too.** One of the driest places on Earth received rare snowfall in 2011 and again in 2013, which stranded travellers and covered desert plants.

19. **There's snow on the equator.** Cayambe volcano in Ecuador sits almost exactly on the equator and its summit, above 5,700 metres, stays under permanent ice and snow. Hawaii's Mauna Kea is snow-capped most winters as well.

20. **A chinook once raised the temperature 49 degrees in two minutes.** On 22 January 1943, in Spearfish, South Dakota, the temperature jumped from −4°F to 45°F in about two minutes, a world record. Within hours it dropped back, cracking windows along the way.

21. **Diamond dust falls from a clear sky.** In very cold, still air, as in Antarctica and the Arctic, tiny ice crystals form near the ground and glitter in sunlight even when no cloud is overhead.

22. **Lake-effect snow can bury a city while the next suburb stays dry.** In November 2014, parts of the Buffalo, New York area got over five feet in three days from narrow bands off Lake Erie, while the city centre only a few miles north saw far less.
