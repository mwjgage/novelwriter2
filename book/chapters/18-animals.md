# Chapter 18: Animals Who Saw the Storm Coming

Animals notice things people can't. Some of that shows up as measurable behaviour. The rest is folklore, and this chapter tries to separate them.

1. **Songbirds left Tennessee before a tornado outbreak in 2014.** Researchers led by Henry Streby tracked golden-winged warblers with tiny geolocators. The birds left their breeding grounds in late April, flew at least 1,500 kilometres, and avoided a system that spawned dozens of tornadoes and killed at least 35 people.

2. **The likely cue was infrasound.** The storms were still 400 to 900 kilometres away when the birds left. Some birds can register sound far below the range humans hear, and the researchers proposed that they detected the low rumble of tornadic storms. It's a **[THEORY]** supported by the evidence, not a settled fact.

3. **Weather radar sees birds.** When flocks of swallows or martins leave a roost at dawn, they appear on radar as rings spreading outward. Meteorologists call them roost rings, and researchers in the 1990s began using them to study populations.

4. **Early radar operators called unexplained echoes "angels."** Many turned out to be birds or insects. Modern dual-polarisation radar can usually tell flocks from rain.

5. **BirdCast turns radar data into migration forecasts.** The project, run by Cornell and partners, predicts nights when large numbers of birds will pass overhead. Cities use it to switch off lights during peak migration.

6. **Bats show up on radar too.** Millions of Brazilian free-tailed bats leave Bracken Cave in Texas on summer evenings, and the cloud is visible to nearby weather radar.

7. **Spiders fly using electricity.** In 2018, researchers Erica Morley and Daniel Robert showed that spiders can launch into the air, a behaviour called ballooning, in response to Earth's atmospheric electric field, even without wind.

8. **Darwin noticed spiders landing on his ship.** During the *Beagle* voyage in 1832, thousands of small spiders landed on the ship about 60 miles off the coast of Argentina, riding on silk threads.

9. **Rain of spiders in Australia in 2015.** In April, residents of Goulburn, New South Wales, reported thousands of spiders descending on silk threads across fields and houses. The event is consistent with mass ballooning after flooding.

10. **Bees reduce foraging before storms.** Honey bees tend to stay closer to the hive when pressure drops and humidity rises. The effect is observed, though the details of what they sense aren't fully worked out.

11. **Blacktip sharks left a Florida bay before a storm.** A 2001 tagging study in Terra Ceia Bay, near Tampa, showed that sharks moved into deeper water ahead of Hurricane Gabrielle. The researchers suggested they sensed the drop in barometric pressure.

12. **Lightning killed 323 reindeer at once.** In August 2016, a single storm struck a herd of wild reindeer on Norway's Hardangervidda plateau. The animals were clustered, and electricity spread through the ground.

13. **Cows lying down means rain is coming is a [MYTH].** Studies of cattle behaviour haven't found that it predicts rain, though herds do change posture with temperature and wind.

14. **Punxsutawney Phil isn't a good forecaster.** The Groundhog Day tradition in Pennsylvania dates back to 1887, and analyses by NOAA and others have put his accuracy somewhere around 35 to 40 percent, which is below a coin flip.

15. **Crickets chirp faster when it's warm.** Amos Dolbear described the relationship in 1897. For snowy tree crickets, count chirps in 13 seconds and add 40 for the temperature in Fahrenheit. It works only roughly, and only for that species.

16. **Toads left a pond before an earthquake in Italy.** In 2009, researchers studying a breeding colony near L'Aquila noticed most males abandoned it days before an earthquake. The link is **[UNCONFIRMED]**, because it's a single observation.

17. **Animal earthquake prediction hasn't been demonstrated.** Reports of strange pet behaviour are common after quakes, but controlled studies have not shown a reliable warning signal.

18. **Hurricanes carry birds off course.** After storms, tropical seabirds and shorebirds turn up far inland, sometimes thousands of kilometres from their usual range. Birdwatchers call these events "wrecks."

19. **Fire ants build rafts when floods hit.** The ants link their bodies into a floating mat that keeps the colony and its queen above water. Photographs of the rafts drifting through flooded Houston neighbourhoods circulated after Hurricane Harvey in 2017.

20. **Seabirds have been used as wind sensors.** In 2016, Japanese researchers published a method using GPS tags on streaked shearwaters, which soar over the sea, to estimate wind speed and direction from the birds' flight paths, which helps fill gaps in ocean observations.
