# Chapter 8: Sprites, Elves, and Ball Lightning

The lightning most people see is the boring kind. Above the storm clouds, other discharges flash for a few thousandths of a second, in red, blue, and rings hundreds of kilometres wide. For decades pilots described them and scientists politely disagreed.

1. **The first picture of a sprite was an accident.** On 6 July 1989, John Winckler of the University of Minnesota was testing a low-light camera for a rocket experiment when it recorded a brief red flash above a distant storm in the frame. It was the first image of a red sprite.

2. **Sprites flash 50 to 90 kilometres up and last a few milliseconds.** They appear above active thunderstorms as red, tentacled columns. Pilots had reported seeing them for decades before cameras caught them.

3. **The name came from folklore.** Researchers at the University of Alaska Fairbanks coined "sprite" in the early 1990s for something that was easy to miss and hard to prove existed.

4. **Blue jets shoot up from the tops of storms.** They climb at roughly 100 kilometres per second to about 40 to 50 kilometres in altitude. They were first recorded from aircraft in 1994.

5. **Gigantic jets reach the ionosphere.** In 2001, a team recorded a jet from a thunderstorm near Puerto Rico that extended roughly 70 kilometres upward and connected the storm to the ionosphere. A study of it appeared in *Nature* in 2002.

6. **ELVES are expanding rings of light.** The name stands for Emission of Light and Very Low Frequency perturbations from Electromagnetic Pulse Sources. They appear at about 90 kilometres and can spread out to roughly 400 kilometres across in a fraction of a millisecond. Japanese researchers recorded them in the 1990s.

7. **A camera on the space station watches for them.** The European Space Agency's ASIM instrument arrived on the ISS in April 2018 to study upper-atmosphere lightning and the gamma rays that sometimes come with it.

8. **Thunderstorms make gamma rays.** Terrestrial gamma-ray flashes were discovered in 1994 by NASA's Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, which was built to look at the cosmos but kept getting hit from below.

9. **They also make antimatter.** NASA's Fermi telescope detected positrons, the antimatter version of electrons, coming from terrestrial gamma-ray flashes in 2011. The finding was published by a team led by Michael Briggs.

10. **Ball lightning was recorded with a spectrograph for the first time in 2012.** A research team on the Qinghai Plateau in China was filming ordinary lightning when a luminous ball appeared after a strike. It lasted about 1.6 seconds, and the spectrum showed silicon, iron, and calcium, elements found in soil. The 2014 paper concluded the ball came from vaporised soil, one **[THEORY]** among many.

11. **Ball lightning has been reported for centuries and is still poorly understood.** Eyewitness accounts describe glowing spheres drifting through rooms or along the ground. Scientists don't agree on a single mechanism, and few events have been recorded by instruments.

12. **A 1638 church storm in Devon may involve ball lightning.** Accounts of the Great Thunderstorm at Widecombe-in-the-Moor on 21 October 1638 describe a fireball entering the church and killing several worshippers. The details come from pamphlets of the time, and the identification as ball lightning is **[UNCONFIRMED]**.

13. **A scientist may have died studying lightning in 1753.** Georg Wilhelm Richmann was killed in St. Petersburg in August 1753 while experimenting with atmospheric electricity. Some accounts describe a ball of fire. The details are secondhand and **[UNCONFIRMED]**.

14. **Volcanoes make lightning.** Ash particles rub together in an eruption plume and build up charge. The "dirty thunderstorm" effect has been photographed at Sakurajima in Japan, Taal in the Philippines, and Iceland's Eyjafjallajökull in 2010.

15. **The Hunga Tonga eruption of January 2022 set the lightning-rate record.** On 15 January, the plume produced about 192,000 flashes over roughly 11 hours, peaking at around 2,600 flashes a minute. Some of the lightning occurred 20 to 30 kilometres up, higher than any seen before.

16. **St. Elmo's fire is a glow, not a flame.** It's a corona discharge: electric fields on a ship's mast or an aircraft windscreen ionise nearby air. Sailors named it after Saint Erasmus, nicknamed Elmo, the patron saint of mariners.

17. **Dry lightning starts fires.** In mid-August 2020, thunderstorms over California delivered more than 10,000 strikes in about three days with little rain. They ignited hundreds of fires, including the large complexes that burned for weeks.

18. **"Heat lightning" is a [MYTH].** The flashes people see on warm nights are ordinary lightning from storms too far away to hear. Thunder usually can't be heard beyond about 15 miles.

19. **Whistlers are radio waves from lightning that travel into space.** They follow Earth's magnetic field lines and return as a falling whistle. Operators on long telephone lines heard them in the nineteenth century, and soldiers heard them on field phones in the First World War.

20. **Lightning keeps the planet humming at 7.83 hertz.** The Schumann resonances, predicted by Winfried Otto Schumann in 1952, are standing waves in the space between the ground and the ionosphere, excited by lightning strikes worldwide.
